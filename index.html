Chemical weapons: history, law, and political consequences

From poisoned tipped arrows to nerve agents, humans throughout history have created a multitude of chemical and biological agents for the use of war.

Ancient civilizations

From poisoned tipped arrows to nerve agents, humans throughout history have created a multitude of chemical and biological agents for the use of war. As early as 10,000 BCE, evidence suggests ancient humans applied poisons derived from insects or animal venoms on arrowheads. Historically, China (approximately 3000 BCE) laced fog with arsenic and Mesopotamians intentionally contaminated water sources.

Later, during 100 CE to the late medieval period, other societies weaponized chemical agents. The Romans extracted cyanide from cherry laurels. Both the Chinese and Mongols burned niter or petroleum to fumigate crude gas-agents. Using a similar concoction, Byzantines famously ignited water sources to burn warships. Throughout this period, arsenic projectiles and stink bombs had also become notorious.

During the First World War, the form of chemical warfare familiar to most of us emerged. Military funded laboratories synthesized chlorine, phosgene, mustard gas, and chloropicrin for the purpose of inflicting mass casualties. In addition, in 1916 the French employed hydrogen cyanide, which marked the first weaponization of its particular kind.

As the war ended a reported 1.3 million soldiers had been injured and 90,000 killed by (CWAs).

Memories of the Horrors of CWAs

The horrors of CWAs endured, so in 1925 an international treaty was signed to ban the usage of CWAs in warfare. However, enforcing the treaty was often difficult, as the treaty stated no significant penalties for CWAs.

Chemical agents leave painful and often fatal wounds on those affected with indiscriminate effects on civilians as well. Historical remedies like herbal treatments were proven ineffective.

The dangers of Chemical Warfare Agents (CWAs)

The dangers of Chemical Warfare Agents (CWAs) remain relevant, and the treatment equally. Treatment mainly falls into 3 categories, mechanical, physical, and chemical decontamination.

However not all CWAs can be treated by same methods. For example, sulfur gas is a more difficult CWA to be treated. Injuries on the skin may need specialized dressings, eye injuries may need lubricants and antibiotics, and lung injuries may need antibiotics, intubation, and surgery.

In addition, some specific treatments are better suited for specific types of CWAs, while others are more effective based on the amount of CWA present. RSDL is more suited for agents that use organophosphates, such as sarin or VX. Compared to Catalyst treatments and metal organic framework that are extreme effective on CWA but only on smaller spills.

Chemical decontamination

Chemical decontamination refers to using reactive substances to change CWAs into less harmful products. 2-PAM and atropine are common antidotes to counter CWAs and an example of chemical decontamination. Super Tropical Bleach, a hypochlorite, reacts to break down contaminants and is used to clean vehicles. Catalyst treatments use catalysts to accelerate the breakdown of CWAs, while metal organic framework treatment capture contaminants and promoting the breakdown of CWAs.

Mechanical decontamination

Mechanical decontamination is defined as removing or reduces contamination without changing the chemical structure of the CWA. Reactive Skin Decontamination Lotion (RSDL), a lotion that absorbs the CWA on contact, but doesn’t react with CWA is an example of this. Fuller’s earth, a clay composed of hydrous chemicals, is used to absorb the CWA. Simply water washing can remove contamination.

Physical decontamination

Physical decontamination is simply removing or covering CWAs physically or using physical agents like UV and heat. Removing articles of clothing from the effect personnel can reduce the risk of CWAs. Physical agents like incineration and high pressure treatments are used to destroy the CWA.
